# Source tiers

How to recognize each tier of evidence, what to exclude, and what to do when a category has no lab testing.

The examples below are illustrative. Reputations change, so confirm a source still tests the way it claims before leaning on it.

## Tier 1: Independent lab testing

**What it looks like:** published, repeatable measurements; a methodology page; many products tested the same way; products bought at retail, or review units disclosed.

**Examples:** Consumer Reports (which also runs reliability surveys); RTINGS (TVs, monitors, headphones, and more); category specialists with measurement labs, such as Notebookcheck for laptops; government test data, such as EPA fuel economy, ENERGY STAR, and IIHS or NHTSA crash ratings for cars.

**Caveats:** lab tests measure a product when it's new. Check that the tests cover the criteria this person cares about; a perfect score on the wrong criteria doesn't help them.

## Tier 2: Expert long-term reviewers

**Signs of a good one:**
- Used the product for weeks or months, not just a launch-week sample
- Compares it directly against competitors
- Discloses how they got the product
- Updates the review when they learn more
- Has a named author with a track record in the category

Testing guides that buy and compare many products belong here. Affiliate links are fine when the testing is real and documented. Affiliate income alone doesn't disqualify a source; lack of testing does.

**Red flags:** a launch-day review from a press sample (useful for first impressions, not durability); no downsides listed; wording that mirrors the press release.

## Tier 3: Owner reports

**Where to find them:** Reddit and category forums, long-term owner reviews, and retailer reviews with detailed text.

**How to weigh them:**
- Specifics outweigh volume.
- The same issue reported by unrelated people over time is a real signal.
- "After six months" or "after a year" reviews are especially valuable.

**Discount:** brand-new accounts, superlatives without specifics, incentivized reviews, and review pages that merge different product versions (common on large marketplaces).

**Search tip:** add terms like "reddit," "long term," "after a year," "problems," or "issues" to the product name.

## Tier 4: Reliability, recalls, and complaints

**Sources:**
- Recall databases: CPSC in the US, NHTSA for vehicles, and their equivalents elsewhere
- Large-sample reliability surveys, such as Consumer Reports'
- Published failure-rate data where it exists, such as Backblaze's hard drive statistics
- Regulator complaint databases, such as the CFPB's for financial products
- Patterns in Better Business Bureau complaints (the complaints themselves, not the letter grade)
- For software and online services: breach and incident history, from the vendor's own security disclosures and from established security or tech news reporting

**How to use them:** mainly for the reliability veto in step 5 of SKILL.md, and as a tie-breaker between close candidates.

## Disqualified

Exclude these entirely:

- "Best X of [year]" lists with affiliate links and no sign the author handled the products: no original photos, no measurements, specs copied from listings
- Content-farm or auto-generated pages: generic text, no author, every category covered
- Sponsored posts and paid placements
- Manufacturer marketing, except for specs (and verify those against test results where it matters)
- Star-rating averages read without the review text
- Reviews of a different model, generation, or regional variant

## Categories with no tier 1

Services (contractors, insurers, software subscriptions, local providers) and many niche products have no lab testing. The best evidence there is usually:

- Specialist reviewers who actually signed up for and used the service
- Detailed owner and customer reports
- Regulator complaint data
- Independent security audits and breach history, for software that holds sensitive data
- License and certification checks, for local trades
- The terms that matter: contract length, cancellation rules, and price increases after promotional periods

Say in the "About the evidence" line that no lab testing existed for the category, so the person knows how much weight the recommendation can bear.
